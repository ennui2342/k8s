What you're seeing

A fisheye lens looking straight up captures the entire sky, horizon to horizon. North is at the top and, because we're looking up rather than down, east is on the left. At night each frame is up to a 15-second exposure, so stars, planets, satellites and meteors all show up.

Keograms

A keogram takes the north–south strip through the middle of every frame and lines them up left to right, so a whole night fits in one picture. Clear spells, clouds rolling in, moonrise and dawn all show as bands. Aurora researchers invented them.

Star trails

Stacking a night's frames shows the Earth's rotation: stars draw arcs around Polaris, near the top of the image. Gaps in the arcs are clouds passing over.

The kit

A ZWO ASI224MC camera with a fisheye lens and dew heater, run by indi-allsky on a Raspberry Pi. Weather comes from a station in the same garden running WeeWX.

Images are kept for 30 days and timelapses for a year.
